Where it's slow, and Windows and macOS

Benchmarks of both renderers at every resolution and effect setting, and a sweep of every sector for its slow spots. Then the same game built and run on a Mac and a Windows laptop.

Every place in every sector green fast · yellow near 60 fps · red slow

Each dot is a spot on a three-cell grid, coloured by its slowest look of four (north, east, south, west). The game-loop time is measured on Linux with an RX 6600M and a Ryzen 7 7840HS. Drag to compare the software renderer (left) with OpenGL at 2160p (right). OpenGL is green everywhere.

What was slow, and what changed

  • Floors and ceilings with heights (software). Since the redesign every sector has heights, and that path called a FUNCTION and divided per pixel. Now each cell is looked up once and the texture row is stepped. 38–50% faster everywhere: Sector 7 went from 57 to 86 fps, Sector 1 from 72 to 100. Same picture.
  • Looking through glass up close (software). The slowest view in the game, at 25–28 ms, because every pixel of the pane is blended over the room behind it. That blend is now written out too, about 20% faster. It's still the worst spot, but under 30 ms.
  • OpenGL has no slow spots. Its frame time barely moves from 480p to 2160p with every effect on. The GPU has over three times the headroom 60 fps needs.

Fixed to make it work everywhere

Code terminals now find qb64pe.exe on Windows by themselves and compile through a batch file there. On Linux and the Mac they use an sh script with plain line endings; the Mac has no timeout, so the program runs without the 5-second limit. macOS's OpenGL warned of an empty texture when phosphor glow was off; it now always gets a picture. The game now lives in its own repository, grymmjack/neon-caster, where GitHub Actions builds Windows, macOS and Linux releases.

New in the engine

  • Floors that bite. Molten and lava floors burn (5 health every half second, with a sizzle and sparks), and murky water or slime slows you to 70% speed. The game decides by the floor picture's name, so any level gets it for free.
  • Monsters keep out of fire and never walk off into a pit with no bottom. Events can't spawn them over one either. Kick one over the edge and it's gone: Over the edge!

New in the level kit

  • Height helpers in lvlkit.py: floor, ceiling, stairs, pit (with steps out), lift and headroom.
  • A height-aware check that walks every level by the game's rules: step up two eighths at most, drop any distance, keep a body's height of headroom, ride lifts and pads, and collect keys in order. It fails a level on an unreachable key or exit, or a stuck spot: anywhere you can get into but can't get out of.
  • A real walk test. A route planner sends an autopilot through each sector using the game's own movement, via its highest and lowest floors and every lift. All ten sectors walk from start to keys to exit.
  • The GPU bench still holds 62.6 fps on every sector, in both CLASSIC and MODERN art.

Classic or modern: every picture painted again

The pixel art stays, and every one of the game's 137 pictures now has a high-resolution twin. Switch between them in SETTINGS > VIDEO > ART, even in the middle of a fight. Drag across any scene below: CLASSIC on the left, MODERN on the right.

Every texture left half classic · right half modern

Every sprite, gun and pickup classic above · modern below

How the art was made

  • Pixelmon on rtx, in full-resolution art mode (SDXL at 1024 pixels), back up and running at about 18 seconds a picture.
  • Guided by the old art. Each painting started from its pixel-art picture (img2img at 0.8 denoise), so the layout, colours and silhouette carry over. A green server wall stays a green server wall, and the gunner keeps his rifle.
  • Walls repeat sideways, floors and ceilings both ways, so they tile seamlessly.
  • Two candidates of everything, 274 renders, picked side by side. The 9 that came out wrong were re-rolled with clearer prompts, such as rock where there should be station panels, or crumpled foil from noisy pixel textures.
  • Sprites are cut out with BiRefNet, a background-removal model on rtx. Each is then fitted into its old picture's exact footprint at four times the size, so monsters stand on the same line and are the same size in the game. Three pieces as grey as the background also used a colour key.
  • 137 pictures, 26 MB, in neon-caster/hd/ under the same names as the pixel art. Delete one and that picture falls back to classic.

How the engine does it

  • Texture size is a variable now. texSize and sprTex are 128 and 64 for CLASSIC, and 512 and 256 for MODERN with OpenGL. Every derived picture follows: key doors, the EXIT sign, the shot-out lamp, corpses, wrecks, hit flashes, explosion frames, and the monsters' walk animation, whose leg swings scale with the picture.
  • ArtReload holds the OpenGL thread off, resizes the arrays, and reloads every picture, including the level's own. If you're jacked in, it rebuilds the network grid at the new size.
  • ReadPicture shrinks a painting by averaging, so the software renderer gets smooth 128-pixel versions, not jagged ones.
  • Paintings are drawn smooth with mipmaps, while pixel art keeps its crisp square pixels, even in MODERN mode where a picture has no painting.
  • No cost. The GPU bench holds 62.6 fps on all ten sectors in both modes.
' in the console, or on the command line
set art 2     ' MODERN
set art 1     ' CLASSIC
01

The level editor

Two views of one level. A CAD-style map from above, and the real 3D game you walk around in while you build. Every edit rebuilds the level in place, so you see the change in the engine the moment you make it.

How a single edit reaches the 3D view

A click, a key or a console command changes the level's text rows (lvlRow$), its zones, lights or lifts. Then GOSUB EditApply runs BuildLevel with editRebuild set. That rebuilds the map arrays the software raycaster reads, the floor and ceiling heights, the glass, the doors, the light map, and every OpenGL mesh: floors, ceilings, walls and raised steps. The GL side swaps in the new meshes as client arrays on the very next frame, without waiting on the GPU, and only bumps the mesh version once the build is finished, so it never draws half a level. Your position, health, monsters and pickups stay put. Ctrl+Z puts the whole previous level back, so every change can be undone.

Getting in F2 · Tab · edit

Press F2 in the game (or type edit in the console, or start the game with +edit). The game pauses and the map view opens on the sector you're in. Tab flips between the map and walking. F2 again goes back to playing, and the level you play is the one you just edited. Ctrl+S writes it to neon-caster/levels/levelN.txt, the same plain-text file the level kit writes.

03

Glass and observation rooms

Your shower idea: windows into rooms you can't reach yet, with computers, chairs, monsters and the dead on the other side. Glass is map glyph =. It takes three hits, cracks first, then shatters and becomes floor.

  • Both renderers draw it. The software raycaster keeps going through a glass cell (GlassRay) and blends the pane over what's behind it (GlassColumn). OpenGL draws the pane as a tinted quad after the opaque world (GLGlass).
  • Floor to ceiling. A pane runs from the cell's floor to its ceiling, however high they are. That fix went in while these shots were taken.
  • Monsters see you through it, and their shots hit the glass and can break it. Nobody walks through it while it stands.
  • Three hits. glassHP counts down with more cracks each time, then shards fall all along the pane and the cell becomes floor. A kick counts, the railgun goes straight through, and a barrel blast takes out every pane near it.
  • Breaking it is loud. The crash wakes every sleeping monster within six cells.
  • Every sector now has observation rooms built by the level kit (levels.py): labs, cells, offices and control rooms with props and bodies placed inside.
04

Data stations and the network

Your other idea from the car: walk up to a station on the wall, press use, and the whole level turns into a bright neon wireframe. Grab the data cores before the timer runs out, dodge the ICE, jack out, and the sealed door opens.

  • The same level, different pictures. JackIn swaps every wall, floor and ceiling texture for generated grid textures (CyberTextures), keeps the real ones aside, and turns the lighting flat and bright. JackOut puts it all back.
  • Data cores are scattered on the floor you can walk to from the station, without going through a door. You need 3 plus one per three sectors, up to 6. The HUD shows NETWORK DATA 0/3 TIME 49.
  • ICE (monster type 10) hunts you down. It's faceted, spiked, and has a white-hot scanning eye.
  • Run out of time and you're thrown out with the door still sealed. Jack in again to retry.
  • Its own music: a synthwave loop at 124 bpm, written for it. Play it below.
05

Floors and ceilings at any height, pits and lifts

Every cell now has its own floor and ceiling height, in eighths of a wall. Steps you can walk up, ledges you have to jump to, pits you fall into, ceilings that soar, and lifts that carry you between them.

  • In the level file, the [floor] and [ceiling] grids give each cell's height: . is the zone's default, 0–9 then a–z are eighths up, and A–H are eighths down (pits).
  • You step up anything under 0.32 of a wall without jumping. Anything higher is a ledge. Your body is 0.7 tall, so you can't fit under a low ceiling.
  • The software renderer draws heights the Doom way: each column keeps a clip window, and step faces, floors and sprites are all clipped to it.
  • OpenGL builds step faces (GLStep) as a fourth mesh kind and rebuilds them when a lift moves.
  • Lifts live in [lifts]: x0, y0, x1, y1, low, high, mode, speed. Mode 1 cycles, 2 is an elevator, 3 waits for a trigger's liftgo.

06

Teleporters

Glyph & is a pad. Pads pair up in reading order: first with second, third with fourth. Step on one and you come out on its partner, facing the way you were going.

  • The pad is a pulsing ring of light on the floor, in both renderers.
  • You have to step off before it sends you back, so no ping-pong.
  • Monsters don't use them, which makes a good escape.
  • A trigger can move you too: tp X Y ANGLE.
07

Triggers and events

A [triggers] section in the level file runs console commands when something happens. Anything you can type in the console, a level can do by itself.

[triggers]
' when [repeat] : command ; command ; ...
enter 10 23 10 23 : message HELLO FROM A TRIGGER ; wall 12 22 . ; light 1 off
use   4 22        : message SWITCH ; door 18 23 open
time  1           : say taunt1
clear 33 33 45 45 : message ROOM CLEAR ; liftgo 1 up
  • enter a box of cells. use a cell (face it, press Space). clear: every monster in a box is dead. time: so many seconds into the sector.
  • Add repeat and it fires every time instead of once.
  • Commands include message, sound, say (a NEXUS line), door, wall, put, light, liftgo, tp, cutscene, jackin and more.
  • Trigger commands run straight away, in the same frame, even while a startup script is still queued. A trigger with a wait in it runs a frame at a time instead, so a trigger can stage a little scene.
08

Code terminals: real QB64 puzzles

NEXUS locks the doors, and the only way through is to repair a program. The lines are shuffled and some words are blanked out. Put them back in order, fill the blanks, press F5, and the game hands it to the real qb64pe, compiles it, runs it, and checks what it prints.

  • Glyph $ is a terminal on the wall. Using it starts a lockdown: every door within seven cells slams and locks, and stays locked until the program runs.
  • Each puzzle teaches one thing, tied to a chapter of the tutorial, in the order the sectors come: PRINT first, the AND 63 texture trick last.
  • Compiling is real. The program is written out with $CONSOLE:ONLY, then qb64pe -x builds it in the background while the screen counts the seconds. The game finds qb64pe by itself.
  • Errors are qb64pe's own, trimmed to the line that matters.
  • Each wrong try reveals a hint. After three, the terminal puts one line right for you.
  • No qb64pe? It checks your lines against the answer instead.

The HUD layout editor U

Press U in the editor (or type hud). The game keeps running underneath, and every HUD element gets a labelled box you can drag. Tab picks the next one, V shows or hides it, C changes its colour, +/- and [ ] size its bar, S saves to hud.txt, R resets, and U finishes.

Under the hood

What changed in the code, how it was tested, and what's left.

Done from the roadmap

  • Door slices audited in both renderers.
  • Dual-view level editor: map and walking, with undo and save.
  • Floor and ceiling heights, pits, ledges and lifts.
  • Teleporters.
  • Triggers and events.
  • HUD layout editor and hud.txt.
  • Monster editor and monsters.txt, with drops.
  • QB64 code puzzles compiled by the real qb64pe.
  • Plus your car ideas: glass observation rooms and data stations with the network.

Still to do

  • Swap the hand-drawn art for Pixelmon renders once rtx's ComfyUI is working again.
  • Deferred on purpose: WAD import, room-over-room and skyboxes.

Fixes found along the way

  • Audio crash. QB64PE's async decode could race and crash. Effects now load with "noasync", and music with "stream, noasync".
  • Half-built GL frames. The mesh version now bumps only when BuildLevel finishes.
  • Mid-game GPU stall. Rebuilt meshes draw from client arrays, so editing never waits on VBO uploads.
  • Glass under high ceilings was drawn only one wall tall in GL. It now runs from floor to ceiling.
  • Quick clicks in the walking editor could be missed between frames. They're now latched, and Ctrl+S/Z work however the key arrives.
  • Screenshots could be skipped when the command queue got ahead of the GL capture. The queue now waits for each one.
  • Plus a new console command, lamp.

How these pictures were made

The GPU shots ran the game on your AMD card through a headless Weston compositor, rendered at double resolution and scaled down. The editor, terminal and HUD shots ran on a virtual X display (Xvfb), with xdotool sending keys and clicks to that display only. Every run used a test build that logs any error and quits, and your desktop was never touched. The GPU benchmark held 62.6 fps on all ten sectors.
